## 1. Executive research conclusion

**Can GPU computing meaningfully accelerate this protocol?** Possibly, but not yet demonstrably: the cloud profiling identifies a large, GPU-shaped workload, and the end-to-end gain is capped. The answer must come from Stage B measurements. What the cloud work establishes:

1. **The dominant tally cost is public verification arithmetic, and it arrives in large independent batches.** [measured CPU]
   * At n = 1,024 a co-located tally performs exactly 130 public variable-base scalar multiplications per conditional gate (2,262,815 in total) plus 7 per gate to finish outputs.
   * These are 79.7 % of all variable-base multiplications in a co-located gate, 94 % for a distributed trustee, and 100 % for the public verifier.
   * They take 59 % of the co-located tally time (37.2 of 63.5 s) and 75 % of the batched public replay (32.2 of 42.8 s).
   * 94 % of these jobs (verification plus finishing) arrive in batches of ≥ 2,048 independent multiplications; the largest is 61,440. That is the right shape for a GPU.
   * At n = 8, no batch exceeds 480 jobs.
2. **None of this work involves secrets.** Every offloaded job is built from transcript values only (points, Fiat–Shamir challenges and responses). Shares, masking signs, simulation randomness and ballot witnesses never reach the device. Accelerating verification therefore needs no new privacy assumption (§6).
3. **A complete GPU-ready implementation exists and is validated as far as possible without a GPU.**
   * Message-level batched verification with a pluggable backend, measured on the CPU at −4.5 % tally time and −8 % replay time at n = 1,024. [measured CPU]
   * A BabyJubjub CUDA kernel, bit-identical to arkworks. [host-executed: 114 vectors and the full protocol]
   * Compiled with NVIDIA's NVRTC 12.9 to sm_89 SASS: 146 registers, 0 spills. [compiled]
   * A cudarc runtime backend [awaiting GPU] and a Windows workflow.
4. **Amdahl caps the end-to-end benefit.** [projection from measured fractions] Even an infinitely fast device would give at most:
   * ≈ 2.2× on the co-located tally (proof production, hashing, decoding and fixed-base work stay on the CPU);
   * ≈ 4.6× on public replay;
   * ≈ 2.9× on a single Groth16 proof, where MSM and FFT are 66 % of 0.92 s.
5. **Against a 24-thread i7-14650HX the RTX 4050's advantage on this 254-bit arithmetic is uncertain.** [projection] A first-order instruction-count estimate puts the unoptimised kernel within roughly 0.5–2× of the CPU's throughput, and an optimised kernel (PTX carry chains) at roughly 2–4×. Only Stage B can decide this.

**Strongest supported conclusion:** a secret-free GPU verification path is technically sound and implemented. The protocol offers GPU-sized batches. The research value lies in the public/secret partition and the batch-aware dispatch (§9), not in raw offloading. Whether the speed-up is material on consumer hardware is **open**. Decision: `MORE_EVIDENCE_NEEDED` (§12).

## 2. Protocol computation analysis

Per conditional gate, each of the five trustees contributes the following. ("var" = variable-base, "fixed" = fixed-base comb with tables for g, h, h̃ and h_i; counts from the code, confirmed by measurement in §3.)

| Message | Producer work (secret-dependent, CPU) | Verifier work per receiving replica (public) |
|---|---|---|
| Mask: X_i, Y_i, e, PoK-CSZ, rerandomization commitment | 5 var + 15 fixed (sign masking, re-encryption, simulated branch) | 5 subgroup checks + 10 var (CSZ) + 10 fixed + 1 hash |
| Reveal: A_i, B_i, ZKP0 | 0 var + 8 fixed | 4 subgroup checks + 4 var + 4 fixed + commitment hash |
| Dec: w_i, DLEQ | 2 var (the share s_i, the DLEQ nonce) + 1 fixed | 1 subgroup check + 2 var + 2 fixed |
| Gate output (everyone) | — | 5 var (λ_i·w_i) + 2 var (halving) |

* **Per gate:** 130 public verification multiplications, 7 public finishing multiplications and 35 secret-dependent ones.
* **Work beyond multiplication:** Ed25519 per message, SHA-256 transcripts, bincode decoding, point decompression and encrypted affine propagation along the DAG.
* **Ballot side:** one Groth16 proof per voter (42,230 R1CS constraints, 37 public inputs) and one Groth16 verification per ballot at admission and in public replay.
* **Sequential dependencies:** the five masking steps of a gate form a chain (R = 7 communication layers per gate). Across gates, the dataflow scheduler exposes up to 32/256/1024/4096 gates at once at n = 8/64/256/1024. Every board message therefore batches many independent gates, which is the source of GPU-sized batches.

**Curve compatibility is decisive.** The tally's arithmetic lives in the BabyJubjub prime-order subgroup:

* base field = BN254 *scalar* field r (254 bits);
* scalars mod q (251 bits);
* twisted Edwards curve, `a = 1` form in arkworks.

ICICLE's BN254 support is for the pairing curve's G1/G2 over the BN254 *base* field, plus NTT over r. Those are exactly the Groth16 prover's operations and none of the tally's. Hence:

* **Direction A (Groth16 MSM/NTT):** ICICLE `icicle-bn254` is the realistic GPU path. Integrating it requires converting the arkworks `ProvingKey` bases (affine G1/G2) and the witness scalars into ICICLE's representation. The `prove_staged` hook in `src/ballot.rs` isolates exactly those calls. Licensing and Windows support make it unsuitable for this open, Windows-reproducible artifact today.
* **Direction B (CGY tally):** no maintained library supports BabyJubjub. A custom kernel is required. The arithmetic is small: one 254-bit Montgomery field and two curve formulas. It can be made bit-identical to arkworks (identical limbs, `R = 2^256`, identical formulas), which makes equivalence testing exact.

**Hardware assumptions (RTX 4050 Laptop, Ada, sm_89).** 6 GB VRAM. A batch job costs 288 B (128 B point in, 32 B scalar, 128 B point out), so even 10^6 jobs need under 300 MB.

## 5. Selected implementation

**Direction B+C: GPU-batched public verification of the CGY tally with adaptive CPU/GPU dispatch.**

Reasons:

1. **Biggest measured cost.** Verification of public proofs is the largest cost in the deployment-relevant setting. In the paper's distributed run, each trustee spent about 168 s verifying versus 15 s producing. The public verifier is verification-only.
2. **Embarrassingly parallel.** The work is independent scalar multiplications that are fully known once a message arrives.
3. **No secrets on the device.** Only public data goes to the GPU, which gives a clean security argument (§6).
4. **Unchanged semantics.** The transcript format, proofs and acceptance predicate do not change, so CPU and GPU verifiers must agree bit-for-bit.

### Architecture

```
board message (trustee batch of items)
   │ decode + Ed25519 signature                   CPU
   ▼
validate(item)   ordering, state, commitments      CPU  (par over items)
proof_jobs(item) + subgroup jobs                   CPU  (par)   ── Job{P, k}
   ▼
MulBackend::run(all jobs of the message)           CPU rayon | CUDA | adaptive
   ▼
proof_ok(item, results)  fixed-base, hashing, ==   CPU  (par)
apply(items); finish(completed gates):             CPU
   λ_i·w_i (5/gate) and Z = (X ± X')/2 (2/gate)    second batch on the backend
```

### Equivalence argument

* The kernel uses arkworks' exact Montgomery representation (R = 2^256) and its exact `add-2008-hwcd` / `dbl-2008-hwcd` formulas.
* It is compared against arkworks' exact double-and-add over the full 256-bit scalar range, including torsion points (the formulas are complete for this curve).
* The device comparison uses canonical compressed encodings.
* Protocol-level equivalence holds by construction: a backend only returns `[k]P` values, and every acceptance decision (hash equality, subgroup result is the identity, the opened value is ±g, 0 or g) is computed on the CPU from those values, exactly as in the inline path.

### Security considerations

* **Secrets never leave the CPU.**
  * `Job`s are created only in `Engine::verify_batched`, `Engine::finish` and the Σ-proofs' `jobs()`; their inputs are transcript points, Lagrange constants, 1/2 and Fiat–Shamir challenges and responses.
  * Trustee production (`Trustee::act`, which uses the share s_i, the masking sign s, r_1, r_2 and the simulated-branch randomness) does not use the backend.
  * GPU memory, driver logs and a shared or cloud GPU therefore see only public data. The privacy statements (Theorem 2, Corollary 1) are unaffected.
* **Soundness depends on correct results.**
  * A faulty or malicious device could make a verifier accept an invalid proof (for example by returning identity for a subgroup check). For the trustees this is a correctness and integrity risk, not a privacy risk.
  * Mitigations (not implemented; proposed):
    * random CPU spot-checks of a fraction ρ of each batch (a single wrong result escapes with probability 1 − ρ per batch);
    * running the independent public verifier on a different backend (CPU); Theorem 1's guarantee is anchored there.
* **Fixed-window kernel is not constant-time.** This is acceptable because it only processes public data. It must not be reused for secret scalars.
* **Generated outputs.** No private keys, shares, witnesses or oracle inputs are stored in `evidence/t005`. All local results go to the ignored `results/local/`.

## 8. Performance interpretation

### Amdahl bounds [projection from §3]

At n = 1,024 (cloud CPU, batched), the offloadable time is the backend multiplications plus batched finishing: 33.3 of 60.7 s for the tally and 33.4 of 42.8 s for the replay. If the device performs that work k times faster than the CPU backend (transfers included):

| k | tally s | S (tally) | replay s | S (replay) |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 60.7 | 1.00 | 42.8 | 1.00 |
| 2 | 44.0 | 1.38 | 26.1 | 1.64 |
| 4 | 35.7 | 1.70 | 17.7 | 2.41 |
| 8 | 31.5 | 1.93 | 13.5 | 3.16 |
| ∞ | 27.4 | 2.22 | 9.4 | 4.57 |

Where each setting sits:

* **Distributed trustees** are close to the replay case: in the paper's Azure run each trustee spent about 168 of 268 s verifying and applying, and 94 % of its multiplications are public. Weak trustee VMs, the configuration that motivated batch 2, are where a GPU matters most.
* **The public verifier** is the best case: 100 % public, with large batches.

### Device-side estimate [projection]

Kernel work per job:

* 2,828 Montgomery multiplications (14 table additions, 256 doublings at 8 multiplications each, 64 additions at 10 each);
* each CIOS multiplication on 32-bit limbs is about 128 32×32→64 multiply-adds plus carry handling, roughly 500–800 SASS instructions with the portable C code;
* total ≈ 1.5–2.3 M instructions per job.

Hardware and expected throughput:

* An RTX 4050 Laptop (20 SMs, 64 INT32 lanes/SM/cycle, about 1.6–2.0 GHz sustained) issues about 2.0–2.6 × 10^12 integer instructions/s, an upper bound of about 1 M jobs/s.
* With the measured register pressure (146 registers, about 25 % occupancy) and 2 KB of local memory per thread, 20–50 % of that is a realistic first expectation: **0.2–0.5 M jobs/s**.
* A 24-thread i7-14650HX running the arkworks wNAF should reach roughly 0.3–0.6 M jobs/s (scaled from the 70 k jobs/s measured on 4 slow vCPUs).
* **So the unoptimised kernel may be roughly at parity with the strong laptop CPU (k ≈ 0.5–2).** PTX `mad.lo/hi.cc` carry chains typically cut multiplication cost 2–3×, giving k ≈ 2–4. These are estimates only.

### Batch size, transfers, synchronisation

* **Bytes moved:** each job moves 288 B. A 61,440-job batch is 17.7 MB, about 1–2 ms at PCIe 4.0 ×8 laptop bandwidth, which is small next to tens of milliseconds of compute. A batch of 64 jobs is dominated by launch and synchronisation latency (tens of µs).
* **Where batches fall:** at n = 1,024, 94 % of the work is in batches of ≥ 2,048, so a threshold around 512–2,048 jobs should suit; `reproduce_gpu.ps1` derives it from the measured sweep.
* **Synchronisation:** the engine synchronises once per board message. Overlapping the next message's decoding with the current batch (double buffering) is a further, unimplemented, optimisation.

### Memory

The largest batch needs about 18 MB of device memory, far below 6 GB. Batches are chunked at 2^20 jobs, about 300 MB.

### Ballot proving (Direction A)

Even a perfect GPU MSM and NTT would leave synthesis and finalize (32 %), so a single proof could speed up by at most 2.9×. Batch proving of 1,024 ballots is embarrassingly parallel on the CPU already. A GPU helps per-ballot latency on weak voter devices more than election throughput. Combined with ICICLE's closed CUDA backend and the lack of a documented Windows build, this is why Direction A was profiled and prepared (`prove_staged`) but not implemented.

## 9. Research novelty assessment

**Plain GPU offloading of scalar multiplication is engineering, not research.** Batched MSM and NTT on GPUs are well studied (ICICLE, cuZK, the ZPrize MSM entries). Our kernel is deliberately simple. Its value is exact equivalence with arkworks, not raw speed.

What *is* specific to this protocol, and not covered by the general GPU-ZK literature we are aware of:

1. **Separation of public and secret work in threshold MPC.** In CGY conditional gates, 137 of 172 variable-base multiplications per gate (79.7 %) are public verification or finishing work in the co-located setting, 94 % for a distributed trustee and 100 % for the public verifier (§2, §3), and none of them touches a share, a masking sign or simulation randomness. That partition is exact and statically checkable: the `Job` type is only ever built from transcript values. This permits *untrusted* accelerators, such as shared cloud GPUs, for the dominant cost, without new trust assumptions for privacy. Soundness still requires the device's results to be correct. Results can be spot-checked on the CPU, and the public verifier can recheck on a different backend. The latter is an instance of verifier diversity, not a cryptographic guarantee.
2. **The protocol dictates the batch shapes.** The size of a batch is set by the protocol's dataflow: message batching across ready gates, the masking chain and the threshold. The measured histograms (§3.D) are heavy-tailed: at n = 1,024, 102 of 912 batches carry 71 % of the jobs. An adaptive CPU/GPU policy therefore has a structural basis, and its crossover can be modelled from device throughput and launch latency.
3. **Batch verification of challenge–response Σ-proofs is not directly possible.** Our proofs hash recomputed commitments. Switching to commitment-form proofs would allow random-linear-combination batch verification: one large MSM per message, typically 3–10× fewer group operations than individual checks, and very GPU-friendly. But it changes the transcript format and needs a security argument:
   * soundness of small-exponent batching under Fiat–Shamir with a challenge derived after the batch is fixed;
   * correct handling of the cofactor-8 torsion of BabyJubjub. Cofactor-cleared batch equations, as in Ed25519 batch verification, would accept transcripts that individual verification rejects. Either a subgroup check of every commitment point, or an argument that torsion in commitments cannot help a cheating prover, is required.

   That is a genuine cryptographic question attached to a measurable systems benefit.

**Assessment.** Items 1 and 2 are a defensible systems contribution once measured: a secret-free GPU verification architecture for threshold-MPC transcripts, with a batch-size-aware dispatch policy. Item 3 is a potential cryptography and systems contribution, but it is unproven. Neither stands on its own without GPU measurements showing a material end-to-end gain in the distributed-trustee or public-verifier setting.

## 10. Potential paper direction

* **Working title:** *Secret-Free Acceleration: GPU-Batched Verification for Threshold Conditional-Gate Tallies*.
* **Research question:** Can the public-verification part of a threshold MPC tally (CGY conditional gates) be offloaded to commodity, untrusted GPUs, with privacy unchanged and soundness kept or checkable, while reducing trustee and verifier time enough to change deployment economics (weak trustee VMs, public auditors)?
* **Three concrete contributions:**
  1. A formal *public/secret job partition* for CGY gates, with an implementation that enforces it by construction, and a security argument: the device sees only transcript data, so privacy is unaffected, and soundness is preserved under CPU spot-checking or verifier diversity.
  2. A GPU kernel bit-identical to arkworks, and an adaptive dispatcher whose threshold follows from a throughput and latency model. It is evaluated on consumer (RTX 4050) and cloud GPUs against multi-core CPUs at n = 8…1024 and beyond, with crossover and Amdahl analysis.
  3. Commitment-form batch verification of the CSZ, ZKP0 and DLEQ proofs, with a cofactor-aware soundness proof, and its measured effect on group-operation counts and GPU time.
* **Central technical innovation:** treat the tally transcript as a *stream of public verification work* whose batch structure is determined by the dataflow schedule, and co-design proof encoding, batching and device dispatch around it.
* **Required security arguments:**
  * privacy is independent of the verification backend (an information-flow argument);
  * soundness with an untrusted device, via probabilistic spot-checking (detection probability per batch) or replicated verifiers;
  * for contribution 3, batched Σ-protocol soundness under Fiat–Shamir with cofactor-8 torsion.
* **Essential experiments:**
  * CPU vs GPU vs adaptive at n = 8 … 4096 (the circuit is linear in n);
  * the distributed trustee setting on weak VMs, with and without GPUs;
  * public-verifier throughput (transcripts per hour);
  * batch-size distributions per scheduler;
  * energy per verified gate;
  * ablations of kernel optimisations and of commitment-form batching.
* **Venues:**
  * systems or blockchain: IEEE ICBC, BlockSys, Middleware, Euro-Par, IPDPS workshops;
  * applied cryptography: ACNS, ESORICS (if contribution 3 is proven), IACR CiC;
  * GPU/HPC: PPoPP or SC workshops (kernel-heavy version).
* **Next milestones:**
  1. Stage B measurements (this handoff).
  2. Kernel v2: PTX carry chains, signed-digit windows, fused subgroup checks.
  3. A GPU-accelerated public verifier as a standalone tool.
  4. A commitment-form batch-verification prototype on the CPU first, then the GPU.
  5. The security write-up.
  6. Cloud-GPU distributed runs.

## 12. Final decision

**`MORE_EVIDENCE_NEEDED`**

1. **GPU suitability.** The profiling establishes a large, secret-free, GPU-shaped workload: 2.26 M independent multiplications per election at n = 1,024, 94 % of them in batches of ≥ 2,048. A complete, validated, GPU-ready implementation exists.
2. **No GPU measurements.** There is no measured GPU speed-up. The decisive quantity, device throughput k relative to a 24-thread i7 on 254-bit Edwards arithmetic, is estimated only, at about 0.5–2× for the unoptimised kernel.
3. **Amdahl caps.** The measured fractions cap end-to-end gains at about 2.2× (co-located tally) and about 4.6× (public replay), even for an infinitely fast device.
4. **Outcome depends on k.** If Stage B measures k ≥ 3–4 at n ≥ 256 (or a clear win on weak distributed trustees or the public verifier), the result upgrades to `VALUABLE_GPU_EXTENSION`. With the batch-verification cryptography of §9 it could become `PROMISING_NEW_PAPER`. If k ≤ 1, the conclusion is `GPU_NOT_BENEFICIAL_FOR_CURRENT_WORKLOAD` for laptop-class hardware, while the CPU batching itself remains a small measured gain.

| Step | Command | Measures |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | discovery | `machine.json`, `nvidia-smi -q`, toolchain, power plan, source revision and dirty flag |

Timing boundaries:

* **Sweep:** around `MulBackend::run`, including packing, transfers, kernel, unpacking and synchronization.
* **End-to-end tally:** from trustee creation to the release decision. It includes the CPU-side proof production; ballot proving is not included.
* **Replay:** a fresh engine processing the whole transcript.
* **Protocol runs:** the existing manifests' definitions (the README's T004 text).

Repetitions:

* sweep: 5 per size;
* end-to-end: 3 alternating-order pairs per n;
* protocol: 3 (`-ProtocolReps`).

The device memory peak comes from the nvidia-smi monitor. `S = T_CPU / T_GPU` and `R = 100 (1 − T_GPU / T_CPU)` are computed from medians.

### J. Research outlook

In order of expected payoff:

1. **The public verifier and weak distributed trustees are the strongest targets.**
   * They are 94–100 % public multiplications with large batches.
   * Amdahl allows up to 4.6× on replay (§8).
   * The paper's Azure trustees spent about 63 % of their time verifying peers' messages.
   * Measure verifier throughput (gates/s, transcripts per hour) and trustee verify time with and without the GPU.
2. **Kernel v2** is where the device's competitiveness is decided (measure v1 first):
   * PTX `mad.lo.cc/madc.hi.cc` carry chains in `fe_mul`, keeping the portable path for host emulation;
   * a signed 5-bit window (8-entry table, halving the 2 KB of local memory);
   * `__launch_bounds__` to reduce the 146 registers and raise occupancy;
   * a dedicated subgroup-check kernel with the fixed NAF of q.
3. **Overlap and adaptive dispatch (Direction C).**
   * Double-buffer: decode and validate message m+1 while message m's batch runs; split a batch between CPU and GPU in proportion to measured throughput.
   * The batch histogram (§3.D) and a two-parameter model (launch latency, device throughput) give a principled threshold. Measuring how well that model predicts the optimum is a small, publishable systems result.
4. **Commitment-form batch verification (cryptography).**
   * Replace per-proof commitment recomputation with one random-linear-combination MSM per message.
   * Fewer group operations even on the CPU, and a natural fit for GPU MSM.
   * Requires a soundness argument that handles BabyJubjub's cofactor 8 (§9) and a transcript-format version bump. It is the most novel direction.
5. **Groth16 on the GPU (Direction A).** Worth doing only for voter-device latency or with an open BN254 G2 MSM. `prove_staged` marks the integration points; Amdahl caps it at 2.9× per proof.
